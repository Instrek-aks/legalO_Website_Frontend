Arbitration, in the context of high -stakes global commerce, is fundamentally chosen for its 
promise of expedited justice, yet disputes  often arise where immediate, pre -tribunal relief is 
necessary to prevent irreparable harm. The figure who grants this relief is the Emergency 
Arbitrator (EA), a creature of institutional rules who can be appointed and pass an order in a 
matter of days.1 While the concept of the EA has been readily adopted by leading arbitral 
institutions worldwide, its legal status in India was, for many years, a source of profound 
doctrinal uncertainty, residing in a twilight zone where it was neither statutorily recognis ed nor 
routinely enforced.  
 
**Legislative Silence and the Duplicative Justice Trap:**
India ’s principal arbitration statute, the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996 (A&C Act), 
remained deliberately silent on the Emergency Arbitrator, a mechanism that was pr oposed but 
ultimately not incorporated into the 2015 amendments. This omission created a gap: an EA 
order, though passed pursuant to institutional rules freely chosen by the parties, did not have a 
clear, direct path to enforcement under the Act. Parties w ho won an EA order were forced into 
a complicated legal workaround, typically having to file an application before an Indian court 
under Section 9 of the A&C Act to obtain an identical, parallel relief, thereby frustrating the 
very essence of quick, party -autonomy -driven arbitration.2 This uncertainty was a significant 
deterrent to parties considering India as an arbitration seat.  
This judicial ambivalence was put to the test  by the Supreme Court in 2021 .3 The Supreme 
Court provided the much -needed clarity by unequivocally holding that an order passed by an 
Emergency Arbitrator in an India -seated arbitration is enforceable under the A&C Act. The y 
achieved this by interpreting the EA order as an interim meas ure granted by an arbitral tribunal 
under Section 17(1) of the Act, which, by virtue of Section 17(2), is deemed to be an order of 
the Court and enforceable as such.4 
The reasoning was the unwavering principle of party autonomy. If the  parties had expressl y 
agreed to the rules of the arbitral institution which contained the provision for an Emergency 
Arbitrator, they were bound by the outcome of that process. The re is no express or implied bar 
in the A&C Act against the enforcement of such orders, thereby v iewing the EA as an important 
step in aid of decongesting the civil courts and affording expeditious interim relief.

**The Incomplete Bridge  and the Path to Parity**
This definitive resolution consciously confined the jurisdiction to India -seated arbitrations, 
utilizing an expansive i nterpretation of the enforcement mechanism under Part I, specifically 
Section 17(2) of the A&C Act. Crucially, this judicial breakthrough did not extend its purview 
to Part II of the Act, which governs foreign -seated arbitrations. This fundamental distinct ion 
arises because EA measures, being inherently provisional and not final awards on the merits, 
fall outside the strict definition of an 'award' enforceable under the New York Convention.6 
Therefore, the same principle of party autonomy that guaranteed sw ift relief domestically 
encounters a structural barrier internationally,  and imposing a clear strategic risk on parties 
seeking urgent protective relief against assets located in India.    
Such foreign EA orders are not directly enforceable as 'foreign awa rds' or 'interim measures' 
under the current statutory scheme. Consequently, a party must still seek relief under Section 
9, using the EA order merely as highly persuasive evidence in an independent court application. 
This dichotomy shows us where legislat ive reform is still needed to align India's practice with 
true global standards and provide a comprehensive, clear mechanism for all emergency arbitral 
orders, irrespective of the seat, thereby ensuring that the legal bridge built is solidified by clear 
statutory mandate.  
This significantly bolstered India's pro -arbitration credentials.  This proved the Indian 
judiciary ’s dedication to upholding party autonomy, transforming the enforceability of EA 
orders into a swift, non -appealable process for domestic arbitrations. This success, however, is  
geographically constrained to Part I. The Section 9  workaround sacrific es efficiency for critical  
protective measures. India ’s strategic goal must be to legislate beyond this judicial fix. Only 
through the explicit statutory recognition proposed in the Draft Bill, extending direct 
enforcement to all seats,  can India genuinely secure its position as a globally consistent 
arbitration hub.
