Introduction
When a normal business collapses, delay burns cash; when a hospital fails, delay harms people.
IndiaΓÇÖs Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC) bring order to corporate distress through the
Corporate Insolvency Resolution Process (CIRP), but the rules were written with factories in mind
rather than intensive care units. A legal moratorium can pause lawsuits and calls from creditors.1
It cannot keep oxygen flowing at night, reassure clinicians that surgeries will start on time, or help
bidders judge clinical risk without mishandling patient records. This essay sets out a
hospitalΓÇæspecific plan that fits within the IBC. It proposes three linked steps: a Clinical Moratorium
to keep emergency care, intensive care, operating theatres, oxygen, blood banks, dialysis, and
diagnostics running while the statutory clock continues;2 PatientΓÇæData Trusts so bidders can
evaluate performance using consented, limited data; and a Central Bank Digital Currency (CBDC)
based microΓÇætranches that pay critical suppliers as soon as a service is verified. The aim is simple:
protect patients, preserve value, and keep the case on time.
Why Hospitals Require a SectorΓÇæSpecific Insolvency Playbook
A hospital keeps its value only when care is safe and uninterrupted. Even a short disruption in the
emergency department, intensive care and operating rooms, oxygen deliveries, blood banking,
dialysis services, or core diagnostics can cancel surgeries, push staff to leave, and lower bids. The
IBC already requires the business to keep running3 and protects essential supplies during the
moratorium.4 However, it defines ΓÇ£essentialsΓÇ¥ as utilities such as electricity, water, telecom, and
IT rather than clinical services. 5 This essay translates those general rules into clear,
hospitalΓÇæspecific steps that a resolution professional (RP) can apply from day one, so patient safety
and financial value rest on the same foundation.

Pillar I: Statutory Clinical Moratorium within the Existing CIRP Timeline
A Clinical Moratorium is a practical rule rather than a loophole or a delay. When CIRP begins, the
RP names the hospitalΓÇÖs critical services, namely emergency care, intensive care, operating
theatres, oxygen and vacuum lines, the blood bank, dialysis, and core diagnostics such as imaging
and laboratory services, and protects these services as strictly as power and water. The timelines
do not change because the case still runs within the IBC limits. The moratorium simply defines
what continuity means in a hospital so that direct patient care is not delayed by legal processes.6
Payments stay strict and predictable. Pay for current use now and route older dues through the
resolution plan. Vendors are paid once the delivery is verified. A short grace window, measured
in hours, prevents paperwork from creating clinical risk, while preserving the vendorΓÇÖs right to
stop new deliveries if current bills are not paid.7
Accountability focuses on results rather than paperwork. If anyone asks the court for more time,
that request should be earned by showing that continuity actually improved. Use three simple
checks. First, were beds and other critical services available when patients needed them? Second,
did cancellations of surgeries and other timeΓÇæsensitive procedures go down? Third, did expected
recovery for creditors improve compared with liquidation value because care continued safely?
These checks keep the focus on what matters: direct patient care and value for stakeholders.8
Pillar II: PatientΓÇæData Trusts for PrivacyΓÇæPreserving Diligence
Bidders cannot price a hospital from revenue and cost lines alone. They also need a clear picture
of the care being delivered, who is treated, what outcomes are achieved, and what kind of risk
exist. Most of this information present inside electronic medical records and related clinical
systems. Because these records are sensitive and governed by consentΓÇæbased law, a neutral
governance layer is required.9

Hospitals run on minuteΓÇæbyΓÇæminute logistics. If an oxygen refill is acknowledged at 6:10 p.m., the
supplier should not wait days to be paid. MicroΓÇætranches close this timing gap by putting a small

the same role. Each clinically important event for example an oxygen cylinder signed in on the

Anticipated Objections and Structured Responses
Privacy worries are addressed by design. The PatientΓÇæData Trust does not centralize records; it
uses explicit consent, narrows access to what is strictly necessary for a short window, logs every
read, and allows revocation. Questions about CBDC readiness are handled by being railΓÇæagnostic:
where digitalΓÇærupee wallets are practical, they are used; where they are not, a programmable bank
escrow achieves the same operational effect. Fears that these mechanisms might slow the case
miss the point. The Clinical Moratorium keeps the matter on the statutory clock by removing
hesitation at the point of care; microΓÇæpayments prevent standoffs with essential vendors that would
otherwise force adjournments; and any extension must be justified with hard numbers rather than
hopeful assurances. Once running, clinicians know supplies will arrive and be paid for, bidders
know what they are buying, and courts see objective indicators rather than ambiguity. Finally,
emergency-care jurisprudence15 and national standards under the Clinical Establishments Act16
reinforce the principle that critical care must not be interrupted, which aligns with the Clinical
MoratoriumΓÇÖs focus on safety during CIRP.
